# Beyond the Blood Bank

## Veterinary Transfusion, Oxyglobin and the Case for a New Oxygen-Delivery Layer

## Abstract

Blood transfusion remains essential in veterinary emergency and critical care. It saves lives and can rapidly restore oxygen-carrying capacity when severe anemia, hemorrhage or hemolysis threatens tissue oxygen delivery. But the donor-blood system depends on a long biological and logistical chain: donor availability, blood collection, typing, compatibility, infectious-disease screening, processing, storage, transport and time.

Those constraints exist even in dogs, one of the best developed veterinary transfusion settings. They become more difficult in cats and horses, and can become fundamental in zoo medicine, wildlife and endangered species where a practical donor system may not exist at all.

The literature also raises a deeper question. The purpose of red-cell transfusion is not simply to increase hematocrit, PCV or hemoglobin concentration. The physiological objective is effective oxygen delivery to tissues.

Large canine transfusion datasets show meaningful survival after pRBC transfusion, but substantial residual mortality. Modern prospective studies also document acute transfusion reactions and rapid post-transfusion alloimmunization. Historical Oxyglobin evidence demonstrates a different therapeutic architecture. In the pivotal FDA field trial, 95% of evaluable Oxyglobin-treated dogs required no additional oxygen-carrying support through 24 hours compared with 32% of controls. All 21 evaluable treated dogs remained free from rescue oxygen-carrying therapy during the first 12 hours. Clinical condition associated with anemia improved significantly at every scheduled post-infusion assessment through 24 hours.

Experimental canine studies measured tissue oxygen tension directly and found that polymerized bovine hemoglobin could restore tissue oxygenation efficiently in severe hemodilution models, in some comparisons producing a faster or greater tissue PO2 response than stored red cells.

These data do not support replacing donor blood as a universal strategy. They support a different question: should veterinary medicine develop an additional donor-independent oxygen-delivery layer for situations where compatible blood is unavailable, delayed or operationally impractical?

---

# 1. Blood transfusion saves lives. That is not the question.

Packed red blood cells and whole blood remain standard veterinary therapies when anemia reduces oxygen-carrying capacity enough to threaten tissue oxygen delivery.

The ACVIM consensus statement on canine immune-mediated hemolytic anemia recommends pRBC transfusion when dogs show clinical features attributable to decreased tissue oxygen delivery. The recommendation is deliberately not based on a single PCV or hematocrit threshold. It considers clinical signs, lactate, speed of anemia progression, expected normal hematocrit and the ability to monitor the patient.

That distinction is important.

We do not transfuse simply because a laboratory number is low.

We transfuse because oxygen delivery has become inadequate or is at risk of becoming inadequate.

The red cell is the physiological vehicle normally used to solve that problem. The clinical endpoint, however, is not the red cell itself. The endpoint is effective oxygen delivery to tissues and organs.

---

# 2. The donor-blood system is much more than a bag of blood

A donor must exist.

The donor has to be available at the right time.

Blood has to be collected safely. It must be typed, screened, processed, stored and transported. Compatibility may need to be established. The correct product must reach the patient before oxygen debt and organ injury progress too far.

Professional veterinary transfusion guidance describes blood as a valuable and limited resource. Canine pRBC products are generally stored for a limited period measured in weeks, not years, and require controlled storage and handling. Transfusion also requires clinical monitoring for reactions.

Dogs have increasingly developed donor programs and commercial blood-bank infrastructure in several countries. But even in dogs, a needed blood product may not be available when an emergency occurs.

For cats, donor availability and blood-group compatibility create additional constraints.

For horses, the donor system is different again.

For zoo species, wildlife and endangered animals, a practical blood bank may be extremely difficult or impossible to maintain.

For a critically endangered population, the potential donor may itself be an animal whose survival and genetic contribution matter.

This is not only a logistics problem.

It can become a biological and numerical problem.

---

# 3. What real-world canine transfusion outcomes show

## Published Evidence

Callan and colleagues reported a large clinical series of 307 transfused dogs. A total of 187 were discharged alive, corresponding to approximately 61% survival to discharge.

A later and much larger study by Hann and colleagues evaluated 3,095 dogs receiving 5,412 packed red-cell units. Across hospital visits, 64.8% were discharged alive. Thirty-day survival after the first pRBC transfusion was 51.9% overall.

The reported 30-day survival differed by major anemia category:

| Clinical category | 30-day survival |
|---|---:|
| Overall | **51.9%** |
| Hemorrhage | **52.1%** |
| Hemolysis | **59.1%** |
| Ineffective erythropoiesis | **44.7%** |

These figures must be interpreted correctly. They do not show that transfusion causes death. The animals were seriously ill for different reasons and the underlying disease strongly affects outcome.

The study also cannot tell us what would have happened to the same dogs without transfusion.

But it demonstrates something clinically important:

**Successful delivery of donor red cells does not guarantee a successful patient outcome.**

A modern randomized leukoreduction trial involving 194 dogs receiving pRBCs reported that 156 survived to discharge, approximately 80.4%. This higher survival illustrates how much outcomes vary with population, disease, center and study design.

There is no single universal veterinary transfusion survival rate.

---

# 4. Derived Analysis: the approximate +20 to 30 percentage-point signal

The BHOC Veterinary discussion of an approximate **+20 to 30 percentage-point net survival gain** should be understood as a comparative analysis of published outcome ranges, not as a single randomized effect size.

Historical severe-anemia and IMHA cohorts reported high mortality in some populations. More recent treatment eras include better transfusion access, intensive monitoring, thromboprophylaxis, immunosuppression, fluid management and other critical-care improvements.

When these published outcome ranges are compared, the difference in survival in selected severe-disease populations can fall in the approximate range of 20 to 30 percentage points.

## Derived Analysis

**Approximate net survival gain: +20 to 30 percentage points across selected published outcome ranges.**

This is not a formal meta-analysis.

It is not a universal transfusion effect.

It should not be interpreted as the expected benefit for an individual dog.

The correct interpretation is that modern transfusion-supported care can materially improve outcome, while substantial mortality remains and the benefit is inseparable from disease severity and the rest of critical-care management.

The detailed analysis framework is maintained separately in:

`analyses/veterinary/canine-transfusion-survival-analysis.md`

# 5. Transfusion complications are measurable

## Published Evidence

A 2024 prospective multicenter study by Hall and colleagues evaluated 858 dogs receiving 1,542 allogeneic blood products.

For pRBC transfusions, the acute transfusion reaction incidence was reported as:

# **8.9%**

A separate randomized clinical trial by Radulescu and colleagues reported acute transfusion reactions in 16 of 194 dogs:

# **8.24%**

These numbers do not make transfusion unacceptable. Donor blood remains essential.

They show that red-cell therapy carries biological complexity that must be monitored and managed.

---

# 6. Compatibility today does not guarantee compatibility tomorrow

Dogs have multiple erythrocyte antigen systems.

Even when the major known antigen is matched, transfusion can expose the recipient to other red-cell antigens.

## Published Evidence

Herter and colleagues prospectively followed previously non-transfused anemic dogs after an initial DEA 1-compatible transfusion.

Within four days:

# **12 of 21 dogs developed a positive crossmatch.**

Additional dogs became incompatible later.

Goy-Thollot and colleagues also documented post-transfusion alloimmunization. Among DEA 1-matched recipients with follow-up, 11 of 25 developed incompatibility against other erythrocyte antigens.

This matters beyond dogs.

Dogs are among the best studied veterinary species. Yet clinically relevant antigen complexity remains.

For species whose blood-group systems are only partly characterized, the uncertainty can be greater.

Their blood biology exists whether or not we have fully characterized it.

---

# 7. Storage is another biological variable

A stored red cell is not a fixed container of hemoglobin.

It is a living cellular product whose biochemical and structural properties change during storage.

In the large Hann study, storage duration was not uniformly associated with mortality across every dog, but important subgroup signals were reported. In dogs with hemolytic disease, longer pRBC storage was associated with lower 30-day survival, with an odds ratio of approximately 0.79 for each additional seven days of storage.

The same study also examined morbidity outcomes including coagulation failure and thromboembolic disease.

This does not mean stored donor blood should not be used.

It means that donor RBC therapy carries variables beyond simply how many grams of hemoglobin enter the circulation.

---

# 8. Oxyglobin introduced a different oxygen-delivery architecture

Oxyglobin was hemoglobin glutamer-200 (bovine), a cell-free polymerized hemoglobin oxygen carrier.

The original FDA indication was treatment of anemia in dogs by increasing systemic oxygen content and improving clinical signs associated with anemia for at least 24 hours, regardless of whether the anemia resulted from blood loss, hemolysis or ineffective erythropoiesis.

Because the product did not contain donor red cells, its use did not require red-cell crossmatching with the recipient.

That represents a fundamentally different architecture.

The oxygen-carrying hemoglobin is present without intact donor erythrocytes.

---

# 9. What the FDA 95% result really means

The pivotal multicenter randomized field trial included 64 client-owned anemic dogs.

Thirty were randomized to Oxyglobin and 34 to untreated control.

The causes of anemia included blood loss, hemolysis and ineffective erythropoiesis.

The endpoint was practical:

**Treatment success meant that no additional oxygen-carrying support was required for 24 hours.**

It was not a survival endpoint.

## Efficacy population

| FDA endpoint | Oxyglobin | Control |
|---|---:|---:|
| Rescue-free through 24 h | **95% (20/21)** | **32% (9/28)** |
| Required additional oxygen-carrying support by 24 h | **5% (1/21)** | **68% (19/28)** |

## Intent-to-treat population

| FDA endpoint | Oxyglobin | Control |
|---|---:|---:|
| 24-h treatment success | **73% (22/30)** | **29% (10/34)** |

The efficacy and ITT comparisons were statistically significant.

The 95% figure must never be labeled as 95% survival.

But it is a strong short-term stabilization signal.

---

# 10. The 5% is easy to misread

The single failure in the Oxyglobin efficacy population does not mean that Oxyglobin had no biological effect in that dog.

It does not mean death.

It means that one dog required additional oxygen-carrying support before the 24-hour protocol window ended.

The time-to-failure data are particularly important.

## Published Evidence

During the first 12 hours:

# **0 of 21 evaluable Oxyglobin-treated dogs required rescue oxygen-carrying support.**

Therefore:

# **100% were rescue-free through the first 12 hours.**

In the control efficacy population, 16 failures had already occurred within the first 12 hours.

Only one Oxyglobin efficacy failure appeared between 12 and 24 hours.

This means the most accurate presentation is:

**100% rescue-free through 12 hours.**

**95% rescue-free through 24 hours.**

The 5% should not be presented as "Oxyglobin did not work in 5%."

The public FDA summary does not provide enough individual-level data to determine the complete clinical trajectory of that one dog before rescue therapy was required.

---

# 11. What happened to the clinical signs of anemia?

The FDA trial did not evaluate only plasma hemoglobin.

It used a Physical Condition Scale that captured clinically important manifestations associated with anemia.

## Published Evidence

At the cohort level, physical condition improved significantly from pretreatment:

- immediately after infusion
- at 4 hours
- at 12 hours
- at 24 hours

The reported significance was p<0.001 at each scheduled post-infusion assessment.

Plasma hemoglobin also increased significantly.

FDA concluded that Oxyglobin increased plasma hemoglobin and improved clinical signs associated with anemia for at least 24 hours.

This permits a strong but precise statement:

# **100% of scheduled post-infusion assessment time points showed significant cohort-level improvement in anemia-associated physical condition.**

It does not prove that every symptom disappeared in every individual dog.

It also does not prove complete elimination of tissue ischemia, because direct ischemia resolution was not the endpoint of this field trial.

---

# 12. Tissue oxygenation studies ask a deeper question

The most interesting evidence may be the studies that measured tissue oxygenation directly.

Standl and colleagues compared a hemoglobin-based oxygen carrier with red-cell transfusion in canine severe-hemodilution models.

## Published Evidence

In the 2003 study, skeletal-muscle tissue oxygen tension increased more rapidly and to a greater degree after HBOC-201 than after stored autologous RBC transfusion in the experimental conditions studied.

The 10th percentile tissue PO2, representing the least oxygenated tissue regions, increased substantially after HBOC administration.

An earlier 1996 study also compared polymerized bovine hemoglobin with stored and fresh red-cell approaches after profound isovolemic hemodilution. The cell-free hemoglobin group restored muscular tissue oxygenation efficiently despite a smaller increase in total circulating hemoglobin than the RBC groups.

These are experimental anesthetized canine models, not clinical survival trials.

But they demonstrate a critical physiological point:

# **Hemoglobin concentration and effective tissue oxygenation are not identical endpoints.**

The amount of hemoglobin in circulation does not by itself tell us how effectively oxygen is reaching poorly perfused tissue regions.

---

# 13. Oxyglobin was directly compared with packed red cells

Zambelli and Leisewitz conducted a prospective randomized clinical comparison in dogs with severe Babesia rossi anemia.

Twelve dogs were randomized to Oxyglobin or packed red-cell transfusion.

Both approaches produced improvements in physiological variables including blood-gas, acid-base and blood-pressure measures.

The study was small and cannot establish equivalence or superiority.

It nevertheless provides an important proof of concept:

**A cell-free hemoglobin oxygen carrier can produce clinically relevant physiological improvement in a setting where packed red-cell transfusion is standard therapy.**

---

# 14. Cats expose the availability problem very clearly

The feline evidence is important because it shows why an alternative oxygen-carrying architecture may matter even when conventional transfusion remains the preferred option.

Gibson and colleagues reviewed 72 cats receiving a hemoglobin-based oxygen-carrying solution.

One of the key operational reasons for use was that compatible feline blood was not readily available.

A later feline series by Weingart and Kohn again documented use of Oxyglobin when feline blood was unavailable or when suitable compatible donors were difficult to obtain, including type B cases.

These studies do not establish universal feline efficacy or safety. The patients were seriously ill and adverse events, including volume-overload concerns, were reported.

The more fundamental message is operational:

# **Clinicians used a cell-free oxygen carrier because the donor-blood system could not always provide the required blood when it was needed.**

---

# 15. Oxyglobin immunogenicity needs a more precise interpretation

A cell-free bovine hemoglobin product is not immunologically invisible.

The FDA target-animal safety study included antibody testing before and after exposure.

Testing was scheduled before treatment and serially afterward.

Low-level canine IgG antibodies against bovine hemoglobin were detected in most Oxyglobin-exposed dogs included in that antibody analysis.

However, the public FDA FOI summary does not provide the individual pretreatment titers for each dog. It also does not provide a detailed individual history of possible prior exposure to bovine hemoglobin-derived therapeutics.

Therefore, the public summary does not allow an independent reader to reconstruct the exact baseline antibody status of every animal.

This is an evidence gap and should be described as such.

It should not be filled with speculation.

---

# 16. Immunogenic does not automatically mean clinically immunotoxic

Hamilton and colleagues studied repeated administration of a bovine hemoglobin-based oxygen carrier in Beagles.

Product-specific IgG developed in most animals after repeated exposure.

But the reported antibodies did not inhibit oxygen binding in vitro, and the study did not demonstrate the systemic allergic or selective immune-complex pathology that would establish clinically important immunotoxicity in that experimental setting.

This distinction matters.

**An immune response can be measurable without automatically producing functional failure or clinical immune injury.**

At the same time, the small experimental study should not be generalized into universal long-term safety.

For next-generation BHOC development, baseline antibodies, longitudinal antibody kinetics, complement activation, renal effects, repeat-dose safety and functional consequences should all be measured explicitly.

---

# 17. Donor RBC transfusion and cell-free hemoglobin create different immune questions

Donor RBC transfusion exposes the recipient to erythrocyte surface antigens and can result in alloimmunization.

Cell-free bovine hemoglobin can induce product-specific antibodies.

These are different immunological mechanisms and should not be presented as equivalent.

The correct scientific question is not:

"Which system is immunogenic and which one is not?"

A better question is:

**What immune response occurs, what is its clinical significance, does it alter oxygen-delivery function, and what happens with repeated exposure?**

That is a testable framework for future development.

---

# 18. What about dogs that received donor blood as well as Oxyglobin?

FDA compared adverse events in dogs receiving Oxyglobin alone with dogs receiving Oxyglobin plus previous and/or supplemental blood transfusion.

The public regulatory summary did not report a statistically significant difference in adverse-event frequency between those groups.

Therefore, the field trial does not support a claim that previous donor blood was proven to reduce Oxyglobin efficacy through increased immune load.

That remains a research question, not an established result.

Previous transfusion can add immunological complexity through alloimmunization, but whether that complexity changes subsequent HBOC efficacy requires direct study.

---

# 19. The control crossover raises a timing question

Of the 34 dogs originally randomized to control, many later received Oxyglobin after additional oxygen-carrying support became necessary.

This matters because those dogs did not begin Oxyglobin at the same clinical moment as dogs randomized to immediate treatment.

They crossed over after deterioration met the protocol rescue criteria.

This creates a clinically important hypothesis:

# **Does waiting for further deterioration carry a biological cost?**

The FDA study was not designed to compare early versus delayed Oxyglobin therapy, so it cannot answer that question directly.

But severe anemia creates a time-dependent oxygen-delivery problem. If oxygen delivery remains inadequate, organs continue to accumulate oxygen debt while treatment is being organized.

Future studies should therefore measure time to restoration of oxygen delivery, not only the final hematocrit.

---

# 20. The human donor-blood system shows why logistics matter

Veterinary blood banking reasonably adapted many principles from human transfusion medicine.

But human medicine has advantages veterinary medicine cannot reproduce at the same scale: millions of potential donors, national collection networks, industrial testing, hospital distribution systems and highly developed blood-bank infrastructure.

Even this system repeatedly experiences shortages.

In August 2024, the American Red Cross reported an emergency shortage after national inventory fell by more than 25% in July.

In July 2026, the Red Cross declared an emergency shortage after supply fell about 25% in June. Two weeks later it declared only the second national blood supply crisis in its history. Type O positive inventory had fallen below a one-day national supply and distribution of type O blood to hospitals was being limited.

This is a sophisticated donor-blood system serving one species.

That makes the veterinary question unavoidable:

# **If donor-blood infrastructure can become critically constrained in human medicine, how realistic is it to reproduce a complete blood-bank system for every animal species?**

---

# 21. We cannot build a blood bank for every species on Earth

Dogs have increasingly sophisticated transfusion programs.

Cats have different blood groups and donor constraints.

Horses require another system.

Other species have different erythrocyte antigens, different physiology and different transfusion knowledge.

Some blood-group systems are well characterized.

Some are partially characterized.

For many wildlife species, clinically practical transfusion knowledge is limited.

Then comes the numerical problem.

When only a small number of animals remain in a threatened population:

# **Who becomes the blood donor?**

The potential donor may itself be endangered.

It may be genetically important.

It may be geographically inaccessible.

Blood collection may require capture, sedation or anesthesia.

Compatibility may be poorly characterized.

The emergency may occur far from a laboratory or blood bank.

This cannot always be solved by building another refrigerator.

---

# 22. Historical Oxyglobin demonstrated a different logistical model

Historical regulatory records describe Oxyglobin as a room-temperature storable cell-free oxygen carrier with a multi-year shelf life and no requirement for red-cell crossmatching.

Those properties belong to the historical product and should not automatically be transferred as proven characteristics of a future BHOC product.

But they demonstrate what becomes technically possible when hemoglobin-based oxygen delivery is separated from intact donor red cells.

A product can potentially be positioned before the donor is found.

That changes the concept of emergency readiness.

---

# 23. The objective is not to recreate whole blood

Whole blood and red cells perform biological functions that a cell-free oxygen carrier does not reproduce.

A future BHOC should not be described as a universal replacement for blood.

The more rational position is:

# **a complementary oxygen-delivery therapeutic**

A bridge when compatible donor blood is unavailable or delayed.

A way to support oxygen delivery while the underlying disease is treated.

A product that could potentially be stored in advance of the emergency.

And, after appropriate species-specific evidence, an option for situations where a full donor-blood infrastructure may never be practical.

---

# 24. The 95% result is more interesting when it is interpreted correctly

The FDA result is often reduced to one line:

**95% versus 32%.**

The deeper pattern is more informative.

In the efficacy population:

- 21 of 21 Oxyglobin-treated dogs remained rescue-free through the first 12 hours.
- 20 of 21 remained rescue-free through 24 hours.
- anemia-associated physical condition improved significantly at every scheduled post-infusion assessment.
- plasma hemoglobin increased significantly.

Meanwhile, most control failures occurred early and many control dogs later crossed over to oxygen-carrying rescue therapy.

Therefore the 5% should not be summarized as "Oxyglobin failed in 5%."

The accurate statement is:

# **One of 21 evaluable dogs required additional oxygen-carrying support between 12 and 24 hours.**

That is a very different endpoint from mortality or complete absence of biological effect.

---

# 25. The evidence does not prove superiority. It justifies the question.

It would be scientifically incorrect to compare:

**95% Oxyglobin treatment success**

with

**51.9% donor-blood 30-day survival**

as though they were the same outcome.

They are not.

The populations are different.

The endpoints are different.

The time horizons are different.

The study designs are different.

But they can be considered together mechanistically.

Together they show:

- donor blood remains lifesaving but is logistically and biologically complex
- survival after transfusion remains strongly influenced by underlying disease
- acute transfusion reactions are measurable
- alloimmunization can develop after transfusion
- cell-free hemoglobin can increase oxygen-carrying capacity without donor red cells
- tissue oxygenation can be measured directly and may reveal information not captured by Hct or Hb alone
- Oxyglobin produced a strong 24-hour rescue-free signal in clinical canine anemia
- a small direct randomized study found physiological improvement with both Oxyglobin and pRBC therapy
- feline clinical use demonstrates the practical importance of an oxygen carrier when compatible blood cannot be obtained

That is enough to justify a serious next-generation research program.

---

# 26. What a next-generation BHOC should prove

A future BHOC cannot rely only on historical Oxyglobin precedent.

It should address questions that earlier programs did not resolve fully.

Species-specific efficacy and dosing must be established.

P50 and oxygen unloading should be characterized.

NO scavenging and vasoactivity should be controlled.

Methemoglobin formation should be monitored.

Renal handling, free-heme biology and oxidative effects should be understood.

Manufacturing purity, endotoxin and pyrogen control should be stringent.

Immunogenicity should be measured before treatment and longitudinally afterward.

Repeat-dose safety needs explicit evaluation.

Most importantly, the clinical program should measure effective oxygen delivery, not only the rise in circulating hemoglobin.

Potential endpoints include:

- systemic DO2
- tissue PO2
- microvascular perfusion
- oxygen extraction
- lactate clearance
- organ-specific perfusion
- time to stabilization
- need for rescue transfusion
- organ dysfunction
- survival

The central question becomes:

# **How quickly and how effectively did oxygen reach the tissues?**

---

# 27. From donor-blood readiness to oxygen-delivery readiness

Veterinary medicine should not abandon donor blood.

It should become less dependent on having only one oxygen-delivery architecture.

The current model asks:

**Do we have the correct blood?**

A future model could ask two questions:

**Do we have compatible donor blood?**

**Do we also have immediate oxygen-carrying support while we obtain it?**

For a dog in a referral hospital, that may add resilience.

For a cat without a compatible donor, it may be critical.

For a horse far from a blood bank, it may change logistics.

For wildlife and endangered species, it may create an option where no realistic blood-bank system exists.

---

# 28. Different blood. One preserved biological requirement.

Different species evolved different red-cell antigens.

Different blood-group systems.

Different physiology.

We should not pretend that every species has the same blood.

But across most vertebrate life, evolution preserved something much more fundamental:

# **hemoglobin-based oxygen transport.**

Nature changed the species.

Nature changed the blood.

Nature changed the antigens.

**Nature kept the oxygen-delivery core.**

That preserved biological function is the foundation worth exploring.

---

# Conclusion

Donor blood saves lives.

The evidence reviewed here does not argue otherwise.

It also shows that donor red-cell therapy depends on availability, collection, compatibility, processing, storage, transport and time. Survival remains strongly influenced by underlying disease. Acute transfusion reactions occur. Alloimmunization can develop rapidly. Compatible blood may simply not be available when needed.

Oxyglobin demonstrated a different oxygen-delivery architecture.

In the FDA efficacy population, 100% of evaluable treated dogs remained free of rescue oxygen-carrying therapy through the first 12 hours, and 95% remained rescue-free through 24 hours. Clinical signs associated with anemia improved significantly at every scheduled post-infusion assessment at the cohort level.

Experimental canine studies demonstrated that cell-free bovine hemoglobin could restore measured tissue oxygen tension efficiently and, in specific models, more rapidly than stored red cells.

A small randomized clinical comparison found physiological improvement with both Oxyglobin and packed red cells in severe canine babesiosis.

Feline experience shows the operational reason this technology matters: sometimes suitable donor blood is simply not available.

None of this proves that an HBOC should replace donor blood.

It supports something more useful:

# **We can build a new layer of oxygen-delivery readiness.**

Not instead of transfusion.

Before transfusion when time matters.

Alongside transfusion when clinically appropriate.

And potentially, after proper species-specific development, where a donor-blood system cannot realistically exist.

# **We cannot build a blood bank for every species on Earth.**

# **But we can build a new layer of oxygen-delivery readiness for all of them.**
